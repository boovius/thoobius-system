`1. Acetate`
What it is: a 2-carbon carboxylate. In cells it is usually handled as `acetyl-CoA`, its activated form.
Why it matters: it is one of the main entry points into fats and membranes. It also feeds the TCA cycle.
From `CO2`: in acetogens and some anaerobes, the `Wood-Ljungdahl pathway` reduces `CO2` to a methyl group and carbon monoxide, then combines them into `acetyl-CoA`. Acetate can be made from that.
How it ends up in life: `acetyl-CoA` becomes fatty acids, lipids, isoprenoids, and many secondary metabolites.

`2. Pyruvate`
What it is: a 3-carbon alpha-keto acid.
Why it matters: this is one of the great crossroads of metabolism.
From `CO2`: a common route is `CO2 fixation` into 3-carbon sugars, then glycolytic steps to pyruvate. In some autotrophs, pyruvate can also be made more directly by enzymes like `pyruvate synthase` or `pyruvate carboxylase`-linked networks.
How it ends up in life: pyruvate feeds alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, acetyl-CoA, fermentation products, and lots of central energy metabolism.

`3. Oxaloacetate`
What it is: a 4-carbon dicarboxylic acid.
Why it matters: it is a key TCA-cycle intermediate and a major branching point for biosynthesis.
From `CO2`: one very direct route is `carboxylation of pyruvate` by `pyruvate carboxylase`, adding `CO2` to make oxaloacetate. Some autotrophic pathways also generate it through reductive TCA chemistry.
How it ends up in life: oxaloacetate becomes aspartate, and from aspartate you get asparagine, methionine, threonine, lysine, and nucleotides.

`4. Succinate`
What it is: a 4-carbon dicarboxylic acid, more reduced than oxaloacetate.
Why it matters: another core TCA/reverse-TCA node.
From `CO2`: in the `reverse TCA cycle`, fixed carbon can flow through oxaloacetate and malate/fumarate into succinate. In some anaerobic metabolisms, succinate is also formed through reductive branches starting from fixed-carbon intermediates.
How it ends up in life: succinate mainly serves as a central metabolic intermediate, helping carbon and electrons move through the network. It supports synthesis indirectly rather than being the main direct precursor of lots of monomers.

`5. Alpha-ketoglutarate`
What it is: a 5-carbon alpha-keto dicarboxylic acid.
Why it matters: this is the main gateway into nitrogen assimilation.
From `CO2`: in the `reverse TCA cycle`, carbon fixation builds toward alpha-ketoglutarate through reductive carboxylation steps. In many organisms it is also reached from isocitrate in standard TCA flow after carbon has already been fixed upstream.
