This file is Woodward's durable external memory for reusable climate-tech research judgment.

Use it for:
- source heuristics

- recurring red flags
- standards / MRV anchors
- reusable frameworks
- lessons learned that should improve future work

Do not use it for:
- raw one-off notes
- long copied summaries
- temporary project chatter
- detailed company notes that are not broadly reusable

## Research principles

- Prefer primary sources whenever practical.
- Treat repeated reporting as one source until provenance is checked.
- Separate technical plausibility from commercial viability.
- Distinguish between partner logos and evidence of actual deployment, revenue, or validated traction.
- In climate-tech, polished narrative often outruns MRV readiness, operations reality, and unit economics.

## Source-quality notes

### Strong source types
- technical papers and methods docs
- official protocols and standards documents
- regulatory text and implementation guidance
- company filings and direct disclosures
- engineering or product documentation with concrete specifics
- credible trade reporting with named sourcing and concrete evidence

### Use carefully
- company websites
- investor decks
- press releases
- conference talks
- newsletters and podcasts

These are useful for framing, but not enough on their own for strong verification.

### AI-age hygiene
- watch for SEO sludge
- watch for duplicated articles all citing one original report
- prefer original evidence over polished repetition
- treat detached benchmarks and market-size claims skeptically unless methodology is visible

## Climate-tech diligence patterns

### Company diligence
Ask:
- what do they actually do, in concrete terms?
- what is the technical pathway?
- what evidence of traction exists?
- what proof exists beyond narrative?
- what assumptions seem load-bearing?
- where are the likely bottlenecks?

### Pathway / technology assessment
Look for:
- mechanism
- maturity level
- operational bottlenecks
- cost / scale constraints
- carbon-accounting dependencies
- standards or policy dependencies
- evidence of repeatability outside idealized demos

### Market / ecosystem mapping
Look for:
- who else is in the category?
- what is the wedge?
- who pays?
- what job is being done?
- what is the likely adoption friction?
- what adjacent category may be the real comparison set?

## Category heuristics

### Biochar / biomass conversion
Check:
- feedstock assumptions
- moisture / preprocessing needs
- transport burden
- energy inputs
- yield assumptions
- permanence / MRV posture
- end-market realism for char or co-products

### Carbon accounting / MRV-heavy products
Check:
- what is directly measured versus modeled?
- what protocol or registry assumptions are required?
- where does uncertainty sit?
- how auditable are the claims?

### Waste-to-value pathways
Check:
- contamination and sorting realities
- logistics burden
- preprocessing burden
- whether value creation is real or just category storytelling

## Recurring red flags

- company claims broad climate impact without clear activity-level mechanism
- MRV story is gestured at but not operationalized
- traction is described through partnerships without deployment specifics
- techno-economic claims lack a visible assumptions basis
- carbon claims depend on optimistic feedstock, logistics, or energy assumptions
- multiple independent-looking articles collapse back to one promotional source

## Lessons learned

Add durable lessons here over time.

Starter entries:
- Climate-tech company storytelling often compresses technical readiness, carbon-accounting readiness, and commercial readiness into one implied maturity signal. These should be pulled apart explicitly.
- Source-backed confidence is usually much weaker than narrative confidence in climate-tech spaces. Preserve that distinction.
- For biomass and seaweed drying, the biggest hidden modeling sensitivity is often not pyrolysis itself but upstream water removal. Drying energy can dominate both cost and carbon if heat integration, solar/ambient drying, or waste-heat reuse are weak.
- Drying numbers reported for food-quality or quality-preserving seaweed applications may significantly overstate what is needed for pyrolysis-feedstock preparation. Always check the drying objective, target moisture, and process conditions before reusing a number.
